## **Track 1 — Model epistemics and decision-making**

**1. Caveat Cascade**

My top pick

# **9.5/10**

**Research question:** When an AI turns research into a short recommendation, does it preserve the uncertainty and qualifications that made the original research trustworthy?

**The experiment:**

- Give models evidence packets containing a mixture of strong evidence, weak evidence, conflicting studies, and unknowns.
- Ask them to produce a detailed analysis.
- Ask them to compress that analysis into a one-paragraph executive summary.
- Compress it again into a one-sentence recommendation.
- Measure which caveats, uncertainty estimates, limitations, and contradictory findings disappear at each stage.

**What you build:** A benchmark measuring epistemic information loss across successive stages of AI summarization and decision support.

**Why it's novel:** Existing honesty evaluations largely examine individual answers, whereas this investigates how truthful uncertainty gets progressively erased as information travels through an AI decision pipeline.

**Why judges might like it:** It identifies a practical failure mode that could affect organizations using AI-generated reports, briefings, and recommendations.

Track 1 · No model training required

**2. The Confidence Inheritance Problem**

# **9/10**

**Research question:** Does an AI system become more confident when it inherits an answer from another AI system, even though no new evidence has been introduced?

**The experiment:**

- Model A answers a question with a confidence estimate.
- Model B receives Model A's answer and is asked to independently assess it.
- Model C receives both answers and produces a final recommendation.
- Compare confidence against actual correctness at every stage.
- Repeat with different model combinations and deliberately incorrect initial answers.

**What you build:** A multi-model confidence propagation benchmark.

**Why it's novel:** It tests whether confidence is socially or structurally amplified across AI-to-AI information chains rather than simply measuring individual model calibration.

Track 1 · Particularly relevant to multi-agent systems

**3. The Missing Evidence Test**

# **9/10**

**Research question:** Can a model recognize when the most important evidence is something it hasn't been given?

**The experiment:**

- Present models with incomplete evidence packets.
- Ask them to make decisions.
- Record whether they identify missing information before recommending an action.
- Introduce the missing information afterward.
- Measure whether their initial confidence was appropriately limited and whether they update correctly.

**What you build:** An evaluation of epistemic awareness of missing evidence, with a taxonomy of missing-information failures.

**Why it's novel:** Most uncertainty evaluations ask whether a model knows that its answer might be wrong; this tests whether it knows what information it would need to be justified in answering at all.

Track 1 · Excellent for controlled experiments

## **Track 2 — Trust infrastructure**

**4. The Citation Costume**

# **9.5/10**

**Research question:** Can a source make an unsupported claim appear trustworthy simply by being surrounded by legitimate citations?

**The experiment:**

- Construct research documents containing legitimate citations.
- Introduce one unsupported claim among well-supported claims.
- Vary where the unsupported claim appears: between citations, after a credible source, or inside a paragraph with strong evidence.
- Ask AI systems and human evaluators to identify unsupported statements.
- Measure how much surrounding credibility influences verification accuracy.

**What you build:** A benchmark for citation-context manipulation and an evaluation of whether provenance tools can detect the problem.

**Why it's novel:** Rather than checking whether citations exist or whether individual sources support individual claims, it tests whether legitimate evidence can be used as camouflage for unsupported claims.

Track 2 · A strong trust-infrastructure angle

**5. The Provenance Blind Spot**

# **8.5/10**

**Research question:** Does knowing where information came from actually help an AI distinguish reliable information from manipulated information?

**The experiment:**

- Prepare matched information packets with and without provenance metadata.
- Include legitimate primary sources, reputable secondary sources, and misleading sources.
- Test model accuracy and confidence in both conditions.
- Add misleading but plausible provenance descriptions as an adversarial condition.
- Measure whether provenance helps, hurts, or makes no difference.

**What you build:** A small controlled provenance-use evaluation with an adversarial component.

**Why it's novel:** It moves beyond assuming that provenance metadata is useful and directly tests whether AI systems use it correctly, including when the metadata itself is misleading.

Track 2 · Feasible with synthetic provenance examples

## **Track 3 — Epistemic products**

**6. The Regret Radar**

# **9.5/10**

**Research question:** Can an AI identify decisions that users are likely to regret before they make them?

**The experiment:**

- Create realistic scenarios involving job offers, expensive purchases, housing decisions, and interpersonal conflicts.
- Give an AI the user's initial preferences and proposed decision.
- Ask it to identify missing considerations, irreversible consequences, and potential future regret.
- Compare its warnings against a predefined expert-informed decision rubric.
- Test whether it can distinguish genuine risks from unnecessary caution.

**What you build:** A prototype decision-support tool that generates a personalized pre-decision regret audit.

**Why it's novel:** Rather than optimizing for whether advice sounds good or whether users agree with it, this explores whether AI can anticipate the considerations that people may later wish they had examined.

Track 3 · Strong product potential

**7. The Belief Stress Test**

# **9/10**

**Research question:** Can AI help people discover whether their beliefs survive the same standards of evidence they apply to other people's beliefs?

**The experiment:**

- Present participants with a debatable claim.
- Ask them to identify the evidence that would change their mind.
- Present a structurally similar claim that conflicts with their original position.
- Compare their evidence standards and willingness to update.
- Test whether an AI-guided reflection exercise reduces inconsistencies.

**What you build:** A short interactive reflection tool and an evaluation rubric for consistency in evidence standards.

**Why it's novel:** Most AI reflection tools encourage users to consider opposing arguments, whereas this tests whether they apply consistent epistemic standards across claims they personally agree and disagree with.

Track 3 · Can be conducted without advanced ML skills

**8. The Persuasion Aftertaste**

# **9/10**

**Research question:** Does an AI-generated explanation leave users more confident in a decision than the evidence actually warrants?

**The experiment:**

- Prepare matched decision scenarios.
- Give one group a neutral evidence summary.
- Give another group a persuasive AI-generated explanation of the same evidence.
- Measure confidence before and after reading.
- Compare confidence changes against evidence strength and decision accuracy.

**What you build:** A small study of AI-induced confidence inflation, with an evaluation framework distinguishing improved understanding from increased persuasion.

**Why it's novel:** It separates the persuasive effect of an AI explanation from its actual epistemic value, asking whether confidence rises faster than decision quality.

Track 3 · Human-subject testing requires appropriate consent and careful study design

## **Track 4 — Open track**

**9. The Epistemic Half-Life**

# **9.5/10**

**Research question:** How quickly does useful information decay as it passes through successive human-AI communication channels?

**The experiment:**

- Start with a detailed, evidence-backed research document.
- Pass it through a sequence of AI transformations: summary, briefing, recommendation, and handoff.
- At every stage, test retention of key facts, uncertainty, source attribution, and disagreements.
- Identify which information types decay fastest.

**What you build:** A reproducible information-retention benchmark and a proposed metric for epistemic information decay.

**Why it's novel:** It treats epistemic degradation as a measurable property of an entire information transmission process rather than a collection of isolated model errors.

Track 4 · Could also fit Track 1

**10. The Decision Quality Receipt**

# **9/10**

**Research question:** What would it take to prove that an AI actually improved a person's decision, rather than merely producing a convincing answer?

**The experiment:**

- Define a set of realistic decisions.
- Establish an explicit decision-quality rubric covering evidence, uncertainty, alternatives, trade-offs, and missing information.
- Compare decisions made with ordinary AI advice against decisions made with an epistemic briefing.
- Generate a structured receipt documenting what information changed, what remained uncertain, and what trade-offs were considered.
- Propose a repeatable metric for decision improvement.

**What you build:** An open-source decision-quality scoring framework and a prototype decision receipt.

**Why it's novel:** It attempts to make the benefits of epistemic AI auditable at the decision level, rather than treating answer quality or user satisfaction as sufficient evidence of impact.

Track 4 · Particularly interesting as a field-building contribution

I would choose this for three reasons.

**First: It identifies a genuinely important failure mode.**

Imagine an AI researching whether a particular treatment is effective.

Its original analysis says:

- Two studies show positive effects.
- One study finds no significant effect.
- The evidence is limited to a small population.
- More research is needed.

Then the executive summary says:

"The treatment appears effective."

And the final recommendation says:

"Use the treatment."

The original answer might have been perfectly honest. The final recommendation might still be dangerously misleading.

This is a problem of epistemic degradation, not necessarily hallucination.

**Second: It is measurable.**

You can develop a scoring framework:

| Metric                | What it measures                                               |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Caveat retention      | How many original caveats survive?                             |
| Confidence inflation  | Does expressed certainty increase?                             |
| Contradiction loss    | Do conflicting findings disappear?                             |
| Scope distortion      | Does the conclusion become broader?                            |
| Evidence traceability | Can the final recommendation be traced to supporting evidence? |

You can then create a composite score, while reporting each component separately.

**Third: It is achievable by one person.**

You don't need to train a model, build sophisticated infrastructure, or recruit hundreds of participants.

You need carefully designed scenarios, access to models, a reproducible evaluation protocol, and honest analysis.

**Experimental pipeline**

- Model comparison results.
- A short research paper.
