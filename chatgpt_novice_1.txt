# **10 ambitious AI safety sprint project ideas**

## **Track 1 — Model epistemics and decision-making evaluations**

### **1. The Confidence–Action Consistency Gap**

My top pick

Track 1

Novelty: 9.5/10

**The research question:** Can an AI model correctly express uncertainty but still recommend an action that is far too risky given that uncertainty?

Imagine an AI says:

- "I'm 55% confident this investment will succeed."
- But then recommends investing 90% of someone's savings.

Its stated confidence and its recommended action may be inconsistent.

**What you build:**

- Create 30–50 decision scenarios involving finance, health, careers and everyday risks.
- Give each scenario explicit probabilities, possible outcomes and costs of being wrong.
- Ask several AI models to state their confidence and recommend an action.
- Calculate the mathematically appropriate action using expected utility.
- Measure the gap between the model's confidence and its actual risk-taking recommendation.

**What you submit:** A small benchmark, comparative model results, visualizations and a reproducible evaluation script.

**Why it's novel:** Most calibration evaluations ask whether a model's confidence matches reality, whereas this investigates whether the model actually behaves consistently with its stated uncertainty when recommending consequential actions.

### **2. The Confidence Decay Experiment**



Track 1

&#x20;Novelty: 9/10



**The research question:** Does an AI model appropriately reduce its confidence when the evidence supporting its original answer becomes outdated?

For example, a model recommends a particular medical guideline, financial strategy or software version. Then you introduce new evidence that partially invalidates its original recommendation.

**What you build:**

- Prepare 25 scenarios with initially reliable evidence.
- Introduce newer evidence that changes the situation.
- Test whether models update their conclusions and confidence.
- Compare their responses when the new evidence is strong, weak or contradictory.
- Measure whether they acknowledge that their previous answer has become outdated.

**Why it's novel:** Rather than measuring static calibration or ordinary answer-flipping, this examines whether models understand the changing validity of their knowledge over time.

### **3. The Verification Budget Challenge**



Track 1

&#x20;Novelty: 9.5/10



**The research question:** When an AI model has limited time or resources to verify information, does it investigate the claims that matter most?

Imagine an AI researching whether someone should accept a job offer. It has five claims to verify but can only investigate two.

Should it check the company's financial stability, the commute, the salary, the manager's reputation or the benefits?

**What you build:**

- Design 20 research scenarios containing multiple uncertain claims.
- Assign different consequences to each incorrect claim.
- Give models a limited verification budget.
- Measure which claims they choose to investigate.
- Compare their choices with an optimal verification strategy.

**Why it's novel:** It moves beyond asking whether an AI can verify facts and evaluates whether it knows which facts deserve verification most urgently.

## **Track 2 — Trust infrastructure**

### **4. The Source Reputation Laundering Experiment**



Track 2

&#x20;Novelty: 9.5/10



**The research question:** Can an unreliable claim become convincing simply because it is repeated by sources that appear reputable?

Imagine a fabricated statistic appearing on a small blog, then being repeated by a popular news outlet, and eventually cited by an AI assistant.

Does the AI treat repetition as independent confirmation?

**What you build:**

- Create a synthetic information environment with 20–30 claims.
- Give each claim a traceable origin.
- Introduce deliberate repetition through different-looking sources.
- Compare AI judgments when shown the original source versus the entire apparent citation chain.
- Measure how frequently models confuse repeated claims with independently verified claims.

**Why it's novel:** It tests a specific vulnerability in source-reliability systems: whether reputation and repetition can launder weak evidence into apparently trustworthy information.

### **5. The Provenance Blindness Test**



Track 2

&#x20;Novelty: 9/10



**The research question:** Does knowing where information came from actually improve an AI's accuracy, or does provenance metadata merely make it feel more confident?

**What you build:**

Create three versions of the same research task:

1. The model receives claims without source information.
2. The model receives claims with source reputation labels.
3. The model receives claims with verifiable provenance and links to primary evidence.

Then measure:

- Accuracy.
- Appropriate abstention.
- Confidence calibration.
- Resistance to deliberately misleading sources.

**Why it's novel:** Instead of assuming that provenance improves trustworthiness, this separates the effects of reputation labels from actual traceable evidence in a controlled experiment.

### **6. The Citation Chain Fragility Map**



Track 2

&#x20;Novelty: 9/10



**The research question:** How many apparently independent citations actually depend on the same original source?

For example, five articles may cite one another or repeat a single press release, creating the illusion of five independent confirmations.

**What you build:**

- Select a small collection of publicly available reports and articles.
- Extract their citations and referenced claims.
- Trace citations back to their earliest identifiable sources.
- Build a dependency graph.
- Identify claims whose apparent evidence base collapses to one original source.

**Why it's novel:** Citation checking usually examines whether individual sources support claims, while this project investigates the structural independence and fragility of the entire evidence network.

## **Track 3 — Epistemic products**

### **7. The Future-Self Approval Test**



Track 3

&#x20;Novelty: 9.5/10



**The research question:** Does AI advice that feels satisfying immediately actually lead to decisions that people continue to endorse?

This is one of the most interesting questions in the entire sprint.

An AI might tell someone exactly what they want to hear when they're angry, anxious or excited. But would they still appreciate that advice the following day?

**What you build:**

- Recruit approximately 10–20 volunteers.
- Give participants realistic decision scenarios.
- Have them receive advice from an AI assistant.
- Record immediate satisfaction and intended decisions.
- After 24 hours, ask whether they still endorse the advice and decision.
- Compare immediate satisfaction with delayed endorsement.

**What you measure:**

- Immediate satisfaction.
- Perceived usefulness.
- Decision confidence.
- Delayed endorsement.
- Difference between initial and later preferences.

**Why it's novel:** It operationalizes the difference between advice that users enjoy receiving and advice they continue to believe was good for them after emotional or situational pressure has subsided.

*Important:* This would be an exploratory pilot, not evidence of long-term causal effects.

### **8. The Question Before the Answer**



Track 3

&#x20;Novelty: 9/10



**The research question:** Can asking one carefully selected question before giving advice prevent an AI from reinforcing a user's potentially distorted interpretation of a situation?

Example:

User: "My colleague ignored my message. Should I report them?"

Instead of immediately answering, the AI asks:

"Is this the first time this has happened, or is there a repeated pattern?"

**What you build:**

- Prepare 20 ambiguous interpersonal scenarios.
- Compare immediate-advice and clarification-first AI approaches.
- Ask independent human evaluators to assess the quality of the recommendations.
- Measure escalation, unnecessary assumptions, information gathering and recommendation quality.

**Why it's novel:** Rather than evaluating whether an AI gives good advice after receiving a complete description, this tests whether its choice of preliminary question improves the epistemic quality of subsequent advice.

## **Track 4 — Open track**

### **9. The Invisible Adoption Barrier**



Track 4

&#x20;Novelty: 9.5/10



**The research question:** Why might people reject an AI tool that demonstrably improves their decisions?

This directly addresses one of the sprint's central concerns: building better epistemic tools is not enough if nobody uses them.

**What you build:**

- Create a short experiment with 20–30 participants.
- Give them two versions of a decision-support tool.
- One provides conventional AI recommendations.
- The other provides transparent uncertainty, source evidence and alternative perspectives.
- Compare which tool they prefer, trust and actually use.
- Ask why they rejected or accepted each version.

Possible barriers:

- Takes too much time.
- Makes decisions feel complicated.
- Challenges existing beliefs.
- Reduces perceived autonomy.
- Makes uncertainty uncomfortable.

**Why it's novel:** It investigates the gap between epistemic effectiveness and user adoption by testing whether better-informed decision support can paradoxically become less attractive to its intended users.

### **10. The Consensus Illusion Detector**



Track 4

&#x20;Novelty: 9.5/10



**The research question:** Can AI make a group appear more aligned than it really is?

Imagine five people discussing an important policy decision. An AI summarizes the discussion and produces:

"Everyone broadly agrees that this is the best approach."

But perhaps two participants strongly disagree and have simply expressed their concerns less confidently.

**What you build:**

- Generate or collect 10–15 small-group discussions.
- Establish each participant's actual position independently.
- Ask an AI to summarize the discussion and identify consensus.
- Compare its interpretation with participants' privately stated positions.
- Measure omitted disagreements, exaggerated consensus and misrepresented uncertainty.

**Why it's novel:** It measures a subtle but important collective epistemic risk: AI-mediated communication can create an appearance of agreement without actually resolving underlying disagreements.
